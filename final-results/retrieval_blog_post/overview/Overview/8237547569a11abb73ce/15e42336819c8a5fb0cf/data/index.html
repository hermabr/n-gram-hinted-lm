How do retrieval hints work?

The context is looked up in the training data on the CPU, and the tokens that followed it there become a hint. On the GPU, the hint is added to the model's residual stream, which raises those tokens' predictions.

Long n-gram hints are rare and reliable

Web text is repetitive, with validation n-grams also appearing in training. For a 20-gram that also appears in training, the most common next token is correct 98% of the time, and 54% for 6-grams. If we record the next tokens of n-grams during training, we can pass them as hints to our model.

How often has the training data seen a context?

For each validation position, its n-gram context is the n tokens ending at it, within its document. A match means the same n tokens occur somewhere in the training data; the vote is the most frequent tokens that followed them there (ties to the lower token id).

How fast can the hint corpus be indexed?

Every version of the hint corpus's index build, from naive Python dicts to the record's, written and timed on the same kind of miso-lo node (48 threads pinned one per core, 800 GB or more per job), each building about the same data: the next token of every position, keyed by its 6-token context, with its 10- and 20-token contexts. Two stages or one pass?

The record builds its hint index in two stages: the scan writes every entry into its partition of an arena, whole cache lines at a time, then each partition is placed into its buckets while its fill counts stay in the CPU's cache. The alternative is one pass: every entry written straight into its bucket in the table as it is hashed. Both are written with AVX-512 and no branches on the data, with the same hash and the same table, and timed as above (48 threads, one miso-lo node per size); hashing alone (reading the tokens, hashing them and computing both buckets, no inserts) is the floor of either.

How does the hint index store a 10-gram?

A simplified hint index (the index of the hint corpus) that keeps 10-grams only. The hash of a 10-gram picks a partition and, in it, two buckets; the 10-gram goes in the first free slot of the emptier of the two. What the slot holds is the rest of the checksum (all of it but the partition's bits) and the next token, 64 bits in all.

How does one table hold the 6-, 10- and 20-gram?

In place of three tables, one per n-gram, the hint index keeps one, keyed on the 6-gram: its hash picks the partition and gives the check. Short fingerprints of the 10-gram's and the 20-gram's hashes ride along in the same entry, so one lookup tells how long the match is.

How much does the size of the hint corpus matter?

One model, trained as the #367 record trains it (seed 0; its training hints come from the tokens it has trained on so far), validated with a hint corpus of the first N training tokens in place of the record's 370 shards. Against it, the data alone: the record trained without hints on N tokens (its model and batch, its schedule stretched to N tokens).

How does the model use a hint?

The hint is added to the residual stream at three places: after the embedding, after layer 6 and before the final norm, each scaled by its own α. What is added depends on the match: its n-gram's length, how often it occurred and how pure its votes were.

Which tokens do the hints help?

The validation tokens grouped by the record's hint at each: none, or the longest n-gram (6, 10 or 20 tokens) whose context matched in the hint corpus. The same record validated with blank hints, and the record trained and validated without hints, show what the model alone gets on those tokens; the previous record (#360) trains 1194 steps to the record's 540.
